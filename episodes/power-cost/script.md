## Opening Hook (0:00-0:30)
[画面：乾陵无字碑特写，缓慢推近碑面空白处，红色裂纹字幕叠加]

This stone is blank.

[画面切到无字碑全景，与右侧唐高宗有字碑对比]

Eight meters tall, a hundred tons of solid granite. Wu Zetian (empress regnant, 624–705 CE) built it herself. Carved every detail — the phoenix at the top, the dragons along the sides. But the center? Nothing. No inscription. No title. No achievements listed.

Three sons, one daughter, a thousand officials, and her own reputation forever.

[画面切到大明宫复原图，镜头掠过宫门]

Every emperor pays a price to rule. But Wu Zetian's price list — the receipts of power that came due — are written in silence, in exile, in poems about birds that could not fly home. This is The Receipts of Power.

## Setup (0:30-1:30)
[画面：武周疆域地图，标注洛阳为神都]

Let's start with what we know for certain. In 690 CE, Wu Zetian proclaimed the Zhou Dynasty and ascended as Holy and Divine Emperor, moving her capital to Luoyang (modern Henan Province). She was 66 years old. The only woman in Chinese history to rule in her own name as emperor (huangdi), not as a regent or an empress consort. She would rule for 15 years — fourteen years and four months, to be precise — until a coup forced her abdication in 705 CE.

[画面：铜匦复原图，四面不同颜色]

To hold that power, she needed tools. In 686 CE, she had copper suggestion boxes installed in Luoyang — four colored boxes, each collecting a different kind of report. One for complaints about corruption, one for military secrets, one for praise of officials, one for criticism of government. But anyone could drop in a tip. No name required. The network spread across the empire. Informants flourished.

[画面：来俊臣画像（素描），《罗织经》书页]

Her most trusted enforcer was Lai Junchen (secret police official, d. 697 CE). He wrote a manual called *The Art of Entrapment* — step-by-step instructions for framing innocent people and fabricating evidence. Zhou Xing (another harsh official, d. 691 CE), was his apprentice. The two of them had so many in their jails that the phrase "please enter the urn" — meaning "you used this trap on others, now it is your turn" — became a permanent part of the Chinese language.

[画面：《大云经疏》书页，与武周官员名单对比]

The tools worked. But power does not come free. And the cost began to collect early.

## Re-hook (1:30-2:00)
[画面：《新唐书》页面，光标圈出"杀女"段，然后划掉]

Now here's the story that has followed her for a thousand years.

The New Book of Tang (1060 CE, Northern Song), written by Confucian historians deeply suspicious of female rulers, says that when Wu Zetian was still a concubine, she smothered her own infant daughter in her sleep and framed Empress Wang for the murder. A move, the story goes, that secured her the throne.

But historians debate this. The Old Book of Tang (945 CE, immediately after the Tang Dynasty fell) — the closest surviving source to her reign — says nothing about this in its main text. The full narrative appears only in the New Book of Tang, compiled 115 years later under a Confucian moral framework that deeply distrusted female rulers.

Modern historians don't believe she killed her baby. The scholarly consensus — from Cen Zhongmian in 1961, Fu Lecheng in 1978, Keith McMahon in 2013 — is that this is a moral fable, not a historical event. A story told by later male scholars about what a woman "must have done" to take power.

So if that wasn't her real first cost... what was?

## Cost Fragments (2:00-3:30)
[画面：科举考场想象图，寒门子弟排队进入]

To balance the harsh tools of secret police and informants, she also built legitimacy from below. In 691 CE, she created the Palace Examination, where she personally interviewed candidates for the highest civil service ranks. Commoners could now register directly for the imperial exams — no noble patron required. She doubled the number of jinshi — the highest degree — awarded each year. Thousands of families who had never held office suddenly entered the bureaucracy.

[画面：龙门石窟卢舍那大佛特写]

And she courted heaven. A group of monks forged a sutra prophesying that a woman would come as the reincarnation of Maitreya Buddha to save the world. Wu Zetian ordered copies distributed across the empire, and temples built in every major city. She donated twenty thousand strings of her personal cosmetic savings to fund the carving of the Vairocana Buddha at Fengxian Temple. Some scholars argue the face of that statue was modeled on hers.

[画面：章怀太子墓壁画，一只孤独的仙鹤飞过]

**First: the poem of the crane.**

Li Xian (Wu Zetian's second son, 655–684 CE) was her heir apparent. Educated, charismatic, popular with the court. In 680 CE, she accused him of treason and exiled him to Bazhou (modern Sichuan Province), a remote corner of the empire. Four years later, a secret police official arrived and forced him to commit suicide. In exile, he wrote a poem — the Yellow Terrace Melons — comparing himself to a melon at the bottom of the vine that would be plucked first. Some scholars argue it was a coded plea: Mother, spare me. He never sent it.

[画面：李显被流放房陵的路线图，从长安到湖北]

**Second: fifty-five days.**

Li Xian (Emperor Zhongzong, 656–710) — the third son, not to be confused with the second — ascended the throne in January 684 CE. His father had just died. He was a grown man, married, experienced. Fifty-five days later, Wu Zetian had him deposed and exiled to Fangling (modern Hubei Province). The reason? He had casually told a court official he might give a government position to his father-in-law. That was enough. He would spend the next fourteen years in a small cottage, his wife with child, guarded night and day.

[画面：狄仁杰画像，眼神平静]

**Third: the loyal turncoat.**

Di Renjie (chancellor, 630–700 CE) was one of the few ministers who both served her Zhou Dynasty and retained the respect of Tang loyalists. Lai Junchen had him imprisoned, sentenced to death, and only a lucky escape saved him. When Wu Zetian later asked Di why he seemed to be biding his time — why he never openly condemned her — he said: "I served your Zhou because you asked me to. But I have always been loyal to the Tang." She did not punish him.

[画面：三个画面拼合——壁画仙鹤、路线图、狄仁杰画像——然后淡出]

Three sons. One of them gone. One of them in exile. One of them forced to forget his name. And all the while, she built her Zhou Dynasty on top of their silences.

## Climax (3:30-6:00)
[画面：酷吏时代受害者名单（部分模糊处理），数字计数器滚动]

The price was not only personal. It was institutional. Lai Junchen and his circle — twenty-seven harsh officials in total — operated a parallel justice system. There were no trials in the modern sense. An informant submitted a tip. A warrant was issued. A family name was entered into the register.

How many died? We don't know the exact number. The official histories mention thousands of households exiled or executed. Some estimates range into the tens of thousands. But the number matters less than the effect: no one felt safe. A private dinner conversation could end a career. A joke about the palace could end a life. The copper suggestion boxes that Wu Zetian had installed to catch corruption became tools of terror.

[画面：狄仁杰与武则天对话想象图（剪影）]

By the late 690s, Wu Zetian knew she had to change. She executed Lai Junchen in 697 CE, when he turned on members of the Wu clan itself. Zhou Xing had already been exiled, killed en route by vengeful families. The informant network was scaled back. The worst of the prisons were closed.

[画面：神龙政变场景想象图，宫门被撞开]

But the damage was done. She could never fully trust her officials again, and they could never fully trust her. The old nobility hated her for breaking every rule. The younger officials owed their careers to her, but still dreamed of the Tang Dynasty they had grown up reading about. Her own Wu clan was fractious, incompetent at governance, riven by jealousy.

[画面：705年2月日历特写，红色圈出20日]

On 20 February 705 CE, the Five Princes — a coalition of generals and ministers — launched the Shenlong Coup. They killed her two closest companions, surrounded the palace, and demanded she abdicate. She was 80 years old. Weakened by illness. She did not fight.

Ten days later, on 3 March 705 CE, her son Li Xian — the one she had exiled for fourteen years — restored the Tang Dynasty. The Zhou was over after fifteen years.

## Resolution (6:00-8:00)
[画面：武则天退位后生活场景想象图（宫中花园，独自散步）]

Wu Zetian spent the last ten months of her life in a small palace pavilion, attended by a single maid. She could no longer leave her bed. She died on 16 December 705 CE. Her final wish: be buried alongside Emperor Gaozong, not as Zhou empress, but as Tang empress consort.

[画面：乾陵全景，无字碑与唐高宗碑并立]

Today, her body rests in a tomb deep inside Qianling Mausoleum, next to Emperor Gaozong (her husband, r. 649–683 CE), the emperor she once served and whose empire she would one day usurp. Beside the memorial path, two stelae stand side by side. On the western one, Emperor Gaozong's achievements are carved in eight thousand beautifully calligraphed characters. On the eastern one — hers — nothing.

Why blank? This is the second great mystery of her reign.

[画面：《唐会要》书页，圈出关于中宗抹去武周的记载]

The dominant academic view points to her son Li Xian. He had been deposed, exiled, humiliated for fourteen years. When he returned to power, he had to restore the Tang legitimacy — which meant erasing the Zhou. The New Book of Tang and the Zizhi Tongjian both frame his reign as a return to proper order. Wu Zetian was now a usurper, not an emperor. An inscription would have acknowledged her reign. A blank stone declared it had never happened.

[画面：龙门石窟卢舍那大佛，然后切无字碑特写]

But there is another story. The popular version says it was her own decision. She ordered no characters carved, so that "later generations may judge me without bias." Modern historians are skeptical — neither the Old Book nor the New Book records such a will — but the image is powerful. A ruler who had controlled every narrative during her reign choosing, in death, to let go.

[画面：713年开元盛世景象（长安城坊市复原图，热闹街道）]

And here is the final cost. She paved the way for one of the greatest eras in Chinese history. Under her, the empire became more meritocratic. More stable. The tax base expanded. The military was reformed. When Emperor Xuanzong — her grandson — finally came to power in 713 CE, he inherited a state far stronger than the one she had seized. That era, called the Kaiyuan Golden Age, is remembered as the peak of Tang civilization.

But Wu Zetian is not in it. Her reforms were attributed to the Tang restoration. Her administrators' achievements were credited to Emperor Gaozong. The Buddhist temples she built were repurposed. The Zhou Dynasty, for its fifteen years, became a footnote — a mistake the Tang had corrected.

## Open Ending (8:00+)
[画面：无字碑特写，从碑顶缓慢下移到空白的中央区域]

This stone is still blank.

[画面：阳光穿过树冠，在碑面上投下移动的光斑]

More than thirteen centuries have passed. Generations of historians have written about her — most with the Confucian moral framework that demands a female ruler be judged more harshly than any male one. Every Chinese emperor of that era made such choices — killing rivals, using spies, exiling family — but for male rulers these were rarely framed as proof of their unfitness to rule. Novelists have retold her story with more drama than evidence. Films have made her into either a monster or a martyr, never quite a person.

[画面：无字碑全景，镜头拉远到乾陵全景，夕阳西下]

We know she did not smother her daughter. We know she wrote poems, loved Buddhist art, cared deeply about finding talented officials from every corner of the empire. We know she made choices that cost tens of thousands of lives. We know she ruled a country that was better for her rule, but hated her for the way she achieved it.

[画面：回到无字碑空白特写，红色裂纹字幕最后一次浮现：THE RECEIPTS OF POWER]

She left us a blank stone. Not a confession. Not a defense. A question, frozen in granite, posed to everyone who comes after her: What would you write here?
